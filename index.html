Core Financial Axiom

"A Rupee Today is Worth More than a Rupee Tomorrow"

In corporate finance, money has a time value. A future sum of money cannot be compared directly to cash today without converting it into its equivalent present worth through the process of Discounting.

1. Purchasing Power (Inflation)

Due to ongoing general price inflation, ₹100 in 5 years will buy significantly fewer goods and services than ₹100 does today. Future purchasing power is diminished.

2. Opportunity Cost of Capital

Having cash today allows you to invest it in risk-free government securities, stocks, or projects to earn a positive return \(r\). Waiting for cash foregoes this earning potential.

3. Default & Uncertainty Risk

The further into the future a cash flow is expected, the greater the uncertainty that the counterparty or market will actually deliver it. Investors demand a risk premium.

Compounding vs. Discounting: Two Sides of the Same Coin

Compounding (Moving Forward) Today → Future

Takes money you have today (PV) and grows it forward into the future by accumulating compound interest at rate \(r\):

$$FV = PV \times (1 + r)^n$$
Discounting (Bringing Back) Future → Today

Takes an expected future cash receipt (FV) and strips out time and risk to discover what it is worth in hand today (PV):

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^n} = FV \times (1 + r)^{-n}$$

What is the "Discount Factor" (DF)?

The expression \(\frac{1}{(1 + r)^n}\) is known as the Discount Factor (DF). It represents the exact present worth of one single unit of currency (₹1 or $1) received \(n\) years in the future.

• As discount rate \(r\) rises → DF drops → PV drops.
• As time period \(n\) extends → DF drops → PV drops.
